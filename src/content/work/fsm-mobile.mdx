## Context

Field service technicians are the people who show up when your air conditioner breaks down in summer, when your office elevator stops working, or when a restaurant's refrigerator fails during dinner service.

When I joined Next Technik in 2021 as the company's first and sole UX/UI designer, I knew nothing about this industry. But looking back, that was actually helpful: I came with no assumptions about how things "should" work.

<div class="figure-row">

My first assignment was redesigning the mobile app these technicians relied on daily. I knew I couldn't design something useful without understanding their world first, so I started with the basics: learning from the internal team about the business model and our products, then talking directly to field technicians about their real world challenges.

## The Problem

The FSM mobile app runs across smartphones, tablets and laptops, with content customisable through NetSuite's Config. Field technicians use it daily to manage tasks, document job details, and fill out forms on site, but the existing UI presented several critical usability issues that impacted their efficiency.

<div class="figure-row">

The app worked, in the sense that jobs could be completed in it. But the interface underneath those jobs hadn't kept pace with how technicians actually used it.

<Figure src="/images/fsm/fsm-02-original-ui.png" alt="Screenshots of the original job list, job detail page and hazard analysis form" caption="The interface before the redesign. Dense, undifferentiated, and built around the data structure rather than the task." mode="contain" />

<div class="process-step__content">

Key research findings, what technicians needed:

<div class="key-points">
<ul>
<li><strong>Easier information finding and updating.</strong> Key information was difficult to locate and scan quickly. Technicians couldn't update incorrect addresses or contact details on site, and needed better search, sort and filter across upcoming and past jobs.</li>
<li><strong>Reduced training requirements.</strong> Technicians struggled with NetSuite jargon like "Project, Case, Task, Job" and unclear statuses like "Pending Approval/Fulfillment". Sync status wasn't visible, and errors gave no way to recover.</li>
<li><strong>Better documentation and preview.</strong> Complex dropdown forms made key fields like "Close Case" easy to miss. No copy and paste, no multi photo upload, and input fields that weren't built for mobile.</li>
<li><strong>Improved communication options.</strong> No way to message between technicians and dispatchers, limited customer communication, and no SMS templates or in-app notifications.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div class="figure-row">

Standard hours 8am to 4pm, always wears glasses out in the field. His pain points: he copies job details into email as a backup in case the app fails to save them, refreshing his job list can take over 40 seconds and he does it multiple times a day, and he spends more time on the phone to the office than actually doing the job.

<summary class="process-step__summary">
<span class="step">4</span>

### Design: four strategies

<span class="process-step__toggle"><span class="process-step__toggle-closed">View more</span><span class="process-step__toggle-open">View less</span></span>
</summary>
<div class="process-step__content">

**Information at your fingertips.** Critical information was hard to find quickly while standing at a job site. Added a date selector for quick day navigation, redesigned task cards with clear hierarchy (priority, deadline, location), and added search, sort and filter. Screens: Task list, Map view, Job details.

**Make the invisible visible.** Hidden sync status and unclear system states caused data loss anxiety and confusion. Added a persistent offline mode indicator, real time sync status with badge counters, and clear error messages with recovery guidance and modal warnings. Screens: Task list, Job details, all forms.

**Forms that work on mobile.** Complex forms led to errors and frustration, especially with gloves on. Split required versus optional fields into tabs, set 44 by 44 pixel minimum touch targets, and added inline validation with prominent save and cancel. Screens: all forms.

**Reduce communication friction.** Phone calls interrupted workflow, and customer communication was limited. Added messaging between technicians and dispatchers, SMS templates for common scenarios, and an in-app notification system. Screens: Task list, new notification screen.

I mapped the current UI flow, pages and components against the existing design files to see what needed reskinning: login, job list, job details, map, and all form components, then redesigned with mobile first principles and documented form guidelines for Customer Service consultants.

### Task summary card

<div class="figure-row">

<Figure src="/images/fsm/fsm-11-task-card-variants.png" alt="Four variations of the task summary card showing different configurations" caption="The same component under four different customer configurations. Priority, status and job identity stay readable in all of them." mode="contain" />

### Form experience

Segmented controls replaced dropdowns, and new form elements were added for name, explanation text, notifications and tooltips, with CTA buttons anchored at the end.

<div class="figure-row">

- 4.5:1 minimum text contrast
- 3:1 minimum UI component contrast
- 44 by 44 pixel touch targets for gloved hands

<Figure src="/images/fsm/fsm-12-form-experience.png" alt="Sales Order and checklist forms showing the new input patterns" caption="Segmented controls, explicit form naming, and actions in a predictable place. The checklist selection screen replaced a flow that previously hid required work." mode="contain" />

</div>

### Design system

The Product Manager wanted a gradual rollout, so Phase 1 was a visual refresh only: no new components, just updating the look and feel to reduce risk. A WCAG AA compliant design system followed: colour system, typography, components, icons, spacing and documentation, for both light and dark themes.

## Outcome

**User testing results.** 9 field technicians, 4 countries, June to July 2022. 8 of 9 rated their overall satisfaction "highly positive." In their words: "It's giving us more of the functionality that we've been asking for a while. More user friendly... it's really good." And on the checklist: "Checklist is much improved. The submit button at the bottom makes much more sense. Like the error message."

**Areas for improvement.** The refresh button's position: users accidentally pressed it while scrolling, so it moved to a fixed top right position. The date selector's visibility: some users didn't notice it, so its visual prominence increased. Map loading feedback: a loading indicator was added to confirm the app was responding on slow connections.

**Quantitative results.** 30+ user interviews across 2 research phases (2021 to 2022). Tested with 27 participants: 18 internal and 9 external users. Overall satisfaction rating: 8/9.

**Business value.** Improved field technician productivity, enhanced user satisfaction across diverse industries, reduced UI related support inquiries, and a scalable design foundation for the features that followed.

## Reflection

**Domain ignorance was an asset, briefly.** Arriving without field service knowledge meant I asked questions people inside the industry had stopped asking. That advantage has a short half life. Within a few months I'd absorbed enough of the internal vocabulary that I had to work deliberately to keep hearing the product the way a new technician would.

**Being the only designer means choosing what not to do.** With one designer, one PM and four developers, the constraint was never ideas. Research surfaced far more opportunity than the team could build, including features like route planning, drag to reschedule, and standalone parts management that customers actively wanted. Writing down what we were deliberately not doing, and why, was some of the most useful work I did.

**The design system outlived the redesign.** The visual refresh was the visible deliverable, but the component library and the consultant facing guidelines were what kept paying off. I'd sequence the guidelines earlier next time, because consultants were shaping the technician experience the entire time I was redesigning screens.

**Instrument first.** The single change that would most improve this case study is data I don't have. On later projects I made analytics a precondition of the redesign rather than a follow up task.

**Accessibility in the field is not office accessibility.** Meeting WCAG AA here meant testing contrast in direct sunlight and in dim plant rooms, and sizing targets for gloved hands. The standard gave us a floor, not an answer. The real work was balancing information density on a small screen against targets big enough to use at arm's length in bad light.
